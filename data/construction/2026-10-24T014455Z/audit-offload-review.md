# RG-062: recorded copy coverage and observed restart

The retained records describe a frozen 136,535-byte copy of a real FreeBSD audit log. The first transfer copied only 65,536 bytes. A later drain copied the remainder. The importer checks the three collector ranges, the final source-file size and cursor, the unchanged source hash, and the actual audit marker found in the collector journal. The earlier partial copy is therefore not counted as complete coverage.

The general proof converts adjacent range checks into coverage of every source-file position. The three ranges are 0–65,536, 65,536–131,072, and 131,072–136,535, with each upper boundary excluded from its range. The proof handles an arbitrary list of ranges. It checks the next boundary at each step, then uses induction to cover every position through the final boundary. A second general result shows that an acknowledgement naming a committed range cannot advance the client cursor beyond the committed data.

The source and collector observations remain distinct evidence. The source readbacks use the same guest connection and exact file/cursor paths. The collector readback binds the process identifier and launch arguments to the observed service restart. Its before/after summaries contain identical ranges, fingerprints, file modes, and full-journal hashes. The same audit marker appears in both summaries. A Lean equality check and the general coverage theorem establish retained range coverage across that one observed restart.

The Python and Go implementation source explains the acknowledgement sequence. The collector checks identity, offset and content hash, appends a record, and flushes the file and parent directory before returning success. The client checks the acknowledged offset and hash before replacing its saved cursor. The importer pins those source files and the collectors that produced the public records. Correct execution, faithful exports, hash behavior, TLS behavior, and source-to-runtime correspondence remain explicit assumptions. Private audit bodies are not exported, so their equality is not reconstructed byte by byte in Lean.

The result contributes technical evidence to GD-15 and GD-18. GD-15 also requires frequent offload; one manually invoked run does not establish a continuing schedule. GD-18 requires an organizational continuity plan for long-term storage. The proof supplies a checked copy mechanism, while adoption and long-term operation remain separate obligations. GD-20 concerns power events. A process restart and successful complete writes do not establish recovery from every possible interrupted write or storage-power failure. GD-13's minimum year and GD-14's quarterly assessments remain open. No whole RG-062 requirement is marked complete.
